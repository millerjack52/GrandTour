# Forest art direction for the LiDAR study

Tyler Smith’s open-world conifer forest is the overall visual target. In the two wide renders, dark irregular trees frame an opening onto cooler, progressively softer ridges. Most of the warmth sits on the peaks and cloud edges. In the playable forest footage the floor is quieter, with a few distinct trunks, rocks and grass silhouettes close to the player. The equivalent view on LiDAR is the moment the pickup turns out of the woods and sees the far bank or paddock tree line. [Sunset forest and playable footage](https://80.lv/articles/open-world-stylized-sunset-forest-scene-made-with-ue5)

David Holland’s *Meadows* supplies the close-range treatment. He paints identifiable grass silhouettes, varies neighbouring clumps together, transfers enclosing normals to tree cards and controls moss on upward-facing stone with a painted mask. His bright meadow palette belongs to his scene. On LiDAR, the same techniques can give the darker woodland a clear silhouette and a detailed ground surface. [Meadows breakdown](https://80.lv/articles/meadows-creating-stylized-nature-in-ue4)

## What to take from the artists' images

**Smith sets the scale of the forest.** The sunset valley plate gives the near conifers distinct, dark outlines. Across the valley, the tree-covered slope reads as one shape. A lighter gap draws the eye towards the mountain and cloud. His earlier Pacific Northwest trail plate achieves depth at walking height between two wooded sides. In that interview he describes arranging gaps between middle-distance stands and fitting the terrain around the large boulders early. In the sunset project he describes painting groups by hand after preparing LODs, and using a spline for shoreline placement. **Holland gives small things a shared pattern.** In his grass comparison, adjacent tufts carry one broad sweep of colour. The small flower and blade silhouettes stay crisp across it. His finished meadow also puts flowers into bands that wrap around the stone. He describes painting simplified plant silhouettes and driving the grass with a low-frequency texture through a restrained colour gradient. On the statue he combines broad baked normals with tiling colour and normal detail, and paints corrections into the upward-facing moss mask. **Kasyanik shows how repetition can follow the terrain.** Watch the moss stay on the upper face while her rock rotates. The same stone can take several orientations and still appear to belong in the meadow. Her grass construction uses several differently shaped clumps. Her forest fog planes, by contrast, were placed for fixed cinematic shots. The LiDAR driver can look back across the same river, so depth needs to work from both directions. ## The next LiDAR study

The original bend broadleaf is the selected tree. Use the existing mature canopy and the daylight palette as the starting point. Build one continuous drive from the wooded approach through the bend to the river opening, with the bank visible past the turn.

1. **Compose the drive in broad shapes.** On the approach, existing near trunks frame one view across the water. Group the noncolliding middle-storey plants into uneven stands on either side of that view. Use the real far slope for the last layer. As the pickup turns, the near trunks should pass across the view and the opening should widen. Test this layout in the original daylight before changing materials. The approach, apex and exit should read distinctly at 320 px in greyscale. Keep the wheel line and sight distance clear.
2. **Build the floor and banks as one surface.** Bake a coarse map from canopy shelter, ground slope and distance to water. Use it to group ferns and litter in sheltered hollows, finer grass at openings, and exposed peat and stone on worn banks. Carry the same broad colour change from soil into the base of the grass. Fit a few partly exposed rock shelves to the bank with visible breaks in profile. Use the existing fine soil and rock normals at close range. Exposed mineral faces and gathered litter should describe the same ledge at pickup height and on foot. These terrain rules can run offline and accept species from another map.
3. **Put the light through the opening.** Start from the preferred colour palette. Seek one side-lit bank where grass tips, mineral faces and bark catch the same warm light, against readable olive shade and a cooler far stand. Check the reverse drive before tuning atmosphere. The earlier low-sun pass made the grass broadly gold, so changing the light alone has already been tested. A baked brush pattern on selected patches of peat is a finishing experiment if the landform and light already read.

Start with the same foliage instance budget and material sample counts. Changing visible coverage can still change GPU time. Compare a matched drive at the usual resolution in each direction, check median and 95th-percentile frame time, and measure base pass and shadows. Carry the rules to a second, previously undressed stretch before making them the forest default.

Smith describes shaping land around boulders, composing mid-distance gaps and using separate texture coordinates for individual leaves and whole-tree gradients in his **earlier Pacific Northwest forest**. These are documented methods for that project and candidate techniques for GrandTour. The sunset feature establishes the visual target. ## Light, scale and cost

Judge the stand first under the saved daylight. Compare a low warm sun from the same bend and open viewpoint. Keep shadow detail in the near olive grass, a cooler middle slope, and a small area of distant warmth. Treat the sky and the distant ridge as a pair. A bright horizon behind every tree would flatten the same layers we want to see.

Implement the layout as reusable stand and edge rules driven by terrain, local canopy and road clearance. Assign roles such as canopy, sapling, groundcover and exposed rock so another map can supply its own species. Carry broad colour and height in mesh data or the existing grass field. Use instancing and distance representations for the repeated shapes. Keep detailed rock and ground normals where the pickup can approach.

## What the first lighting comparison showed

In the running game, the directional sun moved from 24° to 11° above the horizon and from yaw −80° to 54°. The test used 4,050 K and 28,140 intensity, with the saved skylight, fog and exposure. Compared from the same three cameras, the low sun gives the open ridge a clean silhouette and removes the bright strip across the track. The grass also turns a fairly uniform brown-gold and the sky above the ridge is largely empty. On the bend the opposite trees still form a fairly even row. The preferred daylight gives us a consistent way to judge new stand placement and ground materials.

Review the wooded approach, river opening and open skyline from the same cameras. Compare the normal pickup drive in both directions. Check near detail, reduced greyscale silhouettes, transitions to distant foliage, median and 95th-percentile frame time, base pass and shadows. Confirm any cost difference with interleaved repeats. A measured 16.67 ms frame budget is the 60 fps target.
